Premise

The disjoint threat problem

Collective defence holds when the threat is shared. Riyadh insures against Iran. Ankara insures against Israel. Islamabad insures against India. Each member is buying cover from two partners who hold working relations with the adversary in question.

The treaty carries the Article 5 sentence: an armed attack on one is an attack on all. It arrives without an integrated command, a standing force commitment, or an automatic trigger. Riyadh's own spokesman placed the pact outside the category of a military axis within hours of signature. The precedents point the same way. The Gulf Cooperation Council mutual defence provision of 2000 and the Saudi-Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement of September 2025 have both existed without producing Article 5 behaviour.

What follows treats the pact as a set of steps a partner can climb, not an on/off switch. The question in each scenario is which step a partner actually reaches, and which of its other relationships stops it going higher.

Another view to be considered: several regional analysts treat this less as an anti-India pact and more as an anti-Israel one. They see it as a response to fading trust that the United States still provides adequate cover. See Reading the Pact for that case laid out in full.

The asymmetry finding

Pakistan is stuck with this deal. Saudi Arabia and Turkey can walk away from it whenever they like.

Islamabad pays with the one thing it has plenty of: soldiers and aircraft. It cannot say no, because Saudi deposits, deferred oil, and remittances worth six to seven billion dollars a year are the price of saying no. Riyadh and Ankara pay with money and equipment instead, and keep the right to judge each crisis on its own terms before committing to anything more. Pakistan sends troops out. Money comes in. The pact turns the Pakistan Army into hired muscle for Gulf security, paid for in balance of payments support.

This is not a coincidence. It is the pattern. The more tangled up a country is in the wider web of trade, finance, and diplomacy, the less you can count on it to fight. Pakistan has the fewest of those outside ties of the three signatories. That is exactly why it is the one that has already sent troops.

What this costs India

No extra enemy troops show up on the Punjab front because of this pact. Three other things happen instead.

  1. Recapitalisation. Turkish drones, loitering munitions, naval platforms, and Saudi money reach Pakistan in peacetime and sustain it through a short war. The materiel pipeline is the near certain outcome in every India scenario in this model.
  2. Clock compression. Two more capitals now carry a treaty argument for early crisis termination into Washington and Beijing. India's punitive window shortens, which is a planning constraint rather than a battlefield one.
  3. Escalation tolerance. Saudi financial backstopping raises the punishment Pakistan can absorb, which blunts the economic and diplomatic instruments India reaches for below the military threshold.

India's strategy should leverage Saudi Arabia rather than oppose it. Riyadh's economic stake in India far outweighs its ties to Pakistan, and it consistently acts to end crises rather than fuel them. The real challenge is Turkey, which India should counter through defence-industrial moves and Eastern Mediterranean partnerships rather than in the Gulf. Meanwhile, the UAE, whose ties with both Riyadh and Islamabad are cooling, remains an underused strategic lever for New Delhi.

What the aggressor does back

Why the network decides

A signatory gets less willing to fight for a partner the more outside relationships it has to protect. Four mechanisms explain why, and each one works through a country's other relationships, not through the fight directly in front of it. Set the aggressor and victim in the model above to any pairing below and these same four mechanisms reappear as named drivers and brakes.

Mechanism 01

Hegemonic Check

Countries that rely on the United States for their military protection will not attack other US-backed nations, because they cannot afford to jeopardise their own security deal with Washington. Saudi Arabia and Turkey are held back from striking Israel, or from acting against India, not out of fear of those countries themselves. Doing so would anger the US. In the end, dependence on the same powerful patron is the brake that stops these conflicts before they start.

Strongest against Israel as aggressor, any victim, and against India in shorter, lower-stakes conflicts
Mechanism 02

Customer Brake: Commercial Deterrence

A country will not turn against a major economic partner, because doing so would badly damage its own economy. Saudi Arabia stays neutral in an India-Pakistan conflict because Riyadh depends on Indian oil purchases, billions of dollars in shared investment, and millions of Indian workers sending money home. These financial ties act as a hard stop. They block military involvement before it can even start.

Strongest against India as aggressor, Saudi Arabia specifically, across every conflict class
Mechanism 03

Home-Front Limit

A country will not fight on a partner's behalf if doing so opens a new front on its own soil. Pakistan shares a long, unstable border with Iran, has its own Baloch insurgency, and has a large Shia population it cannot afford to alienate. It will help defend Saudi Arabia's airspace, but it will not send troops into Iran itself. The risk of blowback at home outweighs the treaty obligation.

Strongest against Iran as aggressor, and against any actor once the victim shares a land border with the aggressor
Mechanism 04

Token Fulfillment

When a defence treaty has no automatic trigger and no shared command, countries reach for symbolic or indirect support instead: cash, weapons, use of airspace, a vote at the UN, to say they have honoured the pact without actually fighting. This lets a country keep the treaty on paper while staying out of the war and protecting its own wider interests. It is why pacts like the 2000 GCC provision and the 2025 Saudi-Pakistan agreement produce weapons purchases and bank deposits, not army divisions marching to battle.

Holds in nearly every pairing at Sub-conventional, Limited punitive, and Extended conventional class
Fails only where troops are already on the ground and contact becomes unavoidable, Pakistan against Iran being the one live case

Part Five

India's strategic response

01 Blunt the flow 02 Dissuade Saudi 03 Deter Turkey 04 Dissuade Pakistan 05 Own capabilities 06 Six relationships 07 Keep Pakistan embroiled 08 Domestic cost to Army 09 Contest the framing 10 Narrative discipline

Ten tracks, run together rather than in sequence. None of them require India to be a party to the pact, and several work precisely because India stays outside it.

01

Pre-conflict: blunt the standing flow itself

Riyadh's deposits and Ankara's drones are not new capability the treaty created. They are existing bilateral flows the treaty now names. India's leverage sits upstream of the text, not inside it.

Publicly linking Indian pressure to the MJDA is self-defeating. It hands Rawalpindi a grievance narrative. It also forces Riyadh to choose sides in the open, and that works against India, not for it. A public fight forces a real choice. A quiet approach lets Riyadh keep hedging, and hedging favours restraint. The lower-cost move is simpler: keep India's own stake with Riyadh large and visibly growing, the 43 billion dollar trade relationship, 2.7 million workers, the pledged hundred billion dollar investment, the IMEC anchor. Saudi Arabia will then police its own deposits without India having to ask.

Turkey offers no equivalent brake, so the tool has to be different. Extend procurement exclusion, the 2025 Çelebi security-clearance precedent, systematically rather than case by case, across ports, infrastructure, and defence-adjacent contracts. Coordinate quietly with export-control partners on the component supply chains, engines, optics, seekers, that feed the Bayraktar and Akıncı families reaching Pakistan.

Context on scale: Saudi support of roughly eight billion dollars covers a meaningful share of Pakistan's fiscal deficit, itself running around 3.3 trillion rupees. That kind of capital infusion is a temporary survival mechanism for Islamabad, not a path to sustainable prosperity, which is exactly why the flow matters more than any single scenario.

02

Dissuade Saudi Arabia from expanding funding

Riyadh is already the model's clearest brake case. The task is to keep that arithmetic intact, not to threaten it.

Deepen IMEC implementation to the point where Saudi participation is a visible, running asset rather than an announced pledge, a switching cost that grows every quarter it operates. Keep the investment pipeline drawing down, not just promised. Treat the 2.7 million worker relationship and remittance flow as a standing argument in the bilateral channel, not a card played once in a crisis.

Reserve explicit signalling for one threshold only: Saudi financing that moves from balance of payments support to funding specific offensive systems. That distinction is worth making through the existing strategic-dialogue and NSA-level channel, not through public statements, since Riyadh has already done India's work by publicly placing MJDA outside the category of a military axis. Reinforce that framing rather than contest it.

03

Deter Turkey: no Customer Brake, so use a bouquet of other tools

Ankara is, in the model's own words, the one signatory with nothing to protect in New Delhi. It sold Pakistan resupply once already and has no commercial relationship with India large enough to discipline it. Since no single lever does the job, use several at once.

The trade and defence-procurement relationship is thin, so treat it as purely transactional rather than diplomatic: extend the exclusion pattern already begun, keeping it procedural and security-grounded rather than punitive in language. Deepen the existing defence-industrial and diplomatic relationships with Greece, Cyprus, and Armenia, states with their own reasons to raise Ankara's regional costs, since the alignment matrix already marks all three red or amber against Turkey.

Two live grievances worth understanding on Ankara's own side, because they explain its behaviour rather than excuse it. Turkish planners were reportedly upset that IMEC terminates at Haifa and Piraeus with no branch reaching Turkey, and Ankara separately wants to use Pakistan as its route into Central Asia via the Middle Corridor connectivity project. Both point to the same opening: commercial and defence-industrial engagement on India's own terms, supplying engines and auto parts for instance, gives Turkey a reason to calculate rather than simply align with Islamabad.

  • Use India-EU ties. Turkey's own EU accession track and its trade relationship with Brussels give India a second address to raise concerns at, one that does not run through Washington and carries its own weight with Ankara.
  • Use India-US relations. Turkey's NATO membership means Washington already has more leverage over Ankara's behaviour than India does. Align India's concerns with existing American and European unease about Turkish drone exports rather than raising a lone Indian voice.
  • Reduce people-to-people ties. Tourism, education exchanges, and cultural programming are a low-cost, easily reversible signal. Scaling them back costs India little and gives Ankara a visible, deniable-enough consequence.
  • Use Russia. Moscow's own relationship with Ankara, energy, Syria, defence sales, gives India a second channel to shape Turkish behaviour indirectly, through a partner Turkey also has to manage carefully.
  • Enhance defence-tech and production ties with Israel. Israel already shares India's concern about Turkish drone proliferation. Deepen joint development and production specifically in the UAS and loitering-munition space, both to build India's own counter-capability and to signal that Turkey's Pakistan-facing drone business has a cost on the other side of the ledger.

Watch item: the 2028 Turkish election cycle could shift this calculus meaningfully in either direction and is worth tracking on its own timeline.

04

Dissuade Pakistan from adventurism

The asymmetry finding already does most of this work: Rawalpindi is bound, not covered. No case file above puts a Gulf partner in India's theatre.

The task is making that legible to Pakistan's own general staff, not just to outside audiences. Continue the post-2019 and post-Sindoor pattern of demonstrating that limited retaliation draws no outside intervention. Case G's own precedent, Iran's January 2024 strike into Balochistan met with statements only, is the proof of concept and belongs in India's own deterrence signalling, explicitly. Terrorism remains the primary risk to manage here, the elephant in the room that no amount of alliance modelling substitutes for a clear, robust reaction strategy against.

Hold escalation dominance specifically at the sub-conventional and limited-punitive tiers, since that is where the pact's brakes bind hardest: Riyadh mediates rather than arms, Ankara resupplies rather than fights. That is precisely the tier India is most likely to operate at, and precisely where its hand stays freest.

05

Strengthen India's own capabilities

The model names two concrete costs. Both have a specific counter, not a generic one.
  • Recapitalisation. The threat class flowing to Pakistan is named and known: Bayraktar-family UAS, loitering munitions, naval platforms. Counter-drone and counter-swarm investment should be sized against that specific inventory, not against UAS threats in general.

06

Israel, Iran, Afghanistan, the United States, the UAE, the Houthis

Six different relationships, six different jobs. None of them should look coordinated from outside.

Israel. Shared interest in tracking Turkish drone proliferation and in the broader Pakistan-adjacent risk picture. Deepen defence-industrial and intelligence ties on UAS and loitering-munition counters specifically, and keep it quiet. A visibly close India-Israel axis complicates Riyadh's own normalisation file, a relationship India benefits from Riyadh managing carefully, and many Middle Eastern governments are currently critical enough of Israel's regional conduct that overt alignment carries its own cost.

Iran. The neutral-facilitator framing for Case E is correct but underused as a strategic asset. The Iran-Pakistan Home-Front Limit, the 900 kilometre frontier, the Baloch militancy that runs both directions, the Shia population Islamabad cannot offend, is a fact of geography India can lean on without doing anything provocative. Chabahar and INSTC investment give Delhi a legitimate non-military reason to keep deepening the relationship, and every dollar spent there raises Tehran's stake in staying closer to Delhi than to Rawalpindi or Ankara.

Afghanistan. The alignment matrix already marks Kabul red against Pakistan. That is real, low-cost leverage: aid, trade routed through Chabahar, and quiet security dialogue sustain a live second front on Pakistan's western border that India does not have to manufacture, only sustain. Keep it coded as humanitarian and economic engagement to avoid the domestic and international costs of visible embrace.

United States. Every MJDA member except Pakistan buys its security from Washington. The Hegemonic Check is the single most load-bearing mechanism in the whole model. India's job is to keep that fact working in its favour by sustaining its own defence and technology relationship with Washington, so the American incentive to restrain Riyadh and Ankara in any India-Pakistan crisis stays aligned with Delhi rather than neutral, while also watching for daylight between the US administration and its own security establishment, which India can sometimes use.

The UAE. Now the most underused relationship in this list. The matrix correction above puts Abu Dhabi at odds with both Riyadh and Islamabad: a genuine intra-Gulf rivalry with Saudi Arabia, and a cooling, near-transactional relationship with Pakistan following deportations of Pakistani workers and demands for loan repayment. A UAE that acts as an independent, sometimes assertive power in its own right, not simply a junior Gulf partner, is a real opening. Explore a trilateral security and economic conversation involving India, the UAE, and France, which already hosts a base in the UAE and keeps close ties there, as a concrete next step rather than a vague aspiration.

Houthis. Not an Indian instrument and should not become one. India's interest is commercial shipping and energy security through the Red Sea corridor, and the existing maritime-security coordination already covers it. Note for the record only: claims of a Pakistani mechanized division engaging in Houthi-adjacent operations have circulated but remain unverified by this report and should not be treated as established fact.

07

Keep Pakistan embroiled in the Middle East

India cannot order a deployment it has no standing to command. It can shape the incentives at the margin, and largely already has, without trying.

Pakistan's Gulf posture, the squadron and air defence garrisoned in the Kingdom since May 2026, is voluntary and treaty-driven. The honest lever is not Indian pressure on Islamabad but Indian engagement with Tehran: the deeper India's Chabahar and INSTC-adjacent relationship with Iran runs, the harder it becomes for Pakistan to fully disengage from its own western border to focus east, because a military leaning hard into a Sunni Gulf axis against a state it shares 900 kilometres of frontier and a large Shia population with accumulates its own instability on that border independent of anything India does.

The posture to avoid is visible orchestration. If Rawalpindi can plausibly claim India is engineering its Gulf entanglement, that becomes a unifying nationalist narrative rather than a cost. The better position is passive benefit: let the treaty's own asymmetry, Pakistan bound and its Gulf partners optional, do the work, while India's Iran and Afghanistan engagement quietly raises what it costs Rawalpindi to look east instead of west.

08

Make the Gulf commitment domestically costly for the Pakistan Army

The least controllable track, and worth saying so plainly: India cannot compel a sovereign partner-state's deployments. The lever is indirect: amplify a dynamic the model already shows running on its own.

Pakistan already peaked at T4 in the Gulf, the only rung the pact has actually reached, while receiving balance of payments support in return. Manpower out, money in. That is a real domestic story: soldiers garrisoning Saudi Arabia while the home economy runs on IMF conditionality is not an easy trade to defend to a Pakistani public already living with the cost of it.

India should not originate that narrative. A critique landing as visibly Indian in origin is worse than useless inside Pakistan; it converts a real grievance into a loyalty test. The realistic role is to make the underlying facts, deployment numbers, casualty reports, remittance-versus-relief accounting, verifiable and available through open-source tracking that Pakistani press, diaspora commentary, and opposition politics can pick up on their own terms. This dovetails with Track 07: the more bandwidth and domestic political capital the Army spends on Gulf commitments, the less it has for adventurism against India, and the more exposed it becomes to the Baloch and sectarian blowback the Home-Front Limit already predicts.

09

Do not let the civilizational framing go uncontested

Pakistan and Türkiye have separate reasons to cast India's domestic politics as a threat to Muslims rather than a bilateral dispute. Islamabad's reason is obvious. Ankara's is different: Erdoğan's foreign policy has long used the language of Muslim solidarity to position Turkey as a rival seat of Islamic leadership, distinct from Riyadh's, including its own framing as guardian of Islamic holy sites.

This runs on a different logic from the rest of the model. It does not need economic interest to work. Unlike material solidarity, a shared-threat narrative can strengthen a coalition rather than fragment it.

The counter is diplomatic and informational, not domestic; see Track 10 for the domestic complement. Contest specific claims promptly. Do not let them sit uncontested. Lean into the fact that Riyadh, Cairo, and Abu Dhabi have their own incentive not to hand Ankara sole custody of the defender-of-Muslims role. India's task is less about managing one bloc than about keeping the Gulf-Ankara rivalry from resolving in Ankara's favour on this specific narrative.

10

Domestic narrative discipline: make the visibility work for India, not against it

Every foreign-policy choice in this document carries a domestic-visibility cost, and none of them are priced in isolation. Internationally visible domestic communal friction is not separate from the Israel relationship, the Gulf relationship, or the OIC contest in Track 09; it is the raw material that makes all three harder. Pakistan and Türkiye have an identity-based reason to exploit it; China has a purely opportunistic one, since the narrative works whether or not Beijing's own record is consistent with it.

The fact Pakistan and Türkiye need suppressed is not a secret India should be playing defence around. It is a fact India should be putting in front of people. India has the world's second-largest Muslim population, full citizens of a constitutional democracy, not a minority under external protection. That fact does not win the argument by existing quietly in a census table. It wins by being visible.

Concretely: Muslim representation as a standing, not ad hoc, part of the delegations India sends abroad to explain itself, a practice India has used before. Multi-party parliamentary outreach to foreign capitals after past crises has included Muslim MPs specifically for engagement with the Islamic world. Muslim officials, athletes, businesspeople, and cultural figures as a routine part of India's public diplomacy toward the Gulf and OIC-adjacent audiences, not wheeled out only when a crisis needs answering. A counter-narrative that leads with the population figure itself, stated plainly and often, rather than assumed to be self-evident.

This runs alongside, not instead of, minimising the flashpoints: inflammatory rhetoric that reaches international wire coverage, enforcement actions that draw OIC or foreign human-rights attention still cost India every time they happen. But a purely defensive posture concedes the frame to Pakistan and Türkiye by default. The offensive version costs India less than the tracks it protects, and it is the one lever in this document that does not require Delhi to wait for someone else to move first.

Part Six

Reading the pact correctly

Seven things to hold in mind Where our own analysts disagree

Seven things worth holding in mind before treating any single number in this report as settled.

01

It reads like Article 5. It has never behaved like one.

Saudi Arabia has held a mutual-defence clause with the GCC since 2000 and with Pakistan bilaterally since September 2025. Neither has ever produced a NATO-style automatic mobilisation. Expect this pact to follow the same pattern: support that is context-specific, not automatic.

02

This may be an anti-Israel pact wearing an anti-India face.

A recurring reading among regional analysts treats this as substantially a hedge against Israel's expanded operational reach and a growing doubt that the United States still provides adequate security cover, not primarily a vehicle aimed at India. Hold both readings at once rather than picking one.

03

Turkey is competing for religious leadership, not just backing an ally.

Ankara's framing of itself as guardian of Islamic holy sites, its Ummah-leadership ambitions, and its interest in using Pakistan to reach Central Asia through the Middle Corridor all point to a Turkey pursuing its own status contest with Riyadh, not simple solidarity with Islamabad.

04

The nuclear question stays deliberately unresolved.

Ambiguity has value to every party involved. Pakistan has never officially confirmed extending nuclear protection to Saudi Arabia, and Islamabad's own defence establishment has said nuclear weapons are not on the radar of this pact, while also declining to rule out that its capabilities generally would be available. Treat the nuclear dimension as unresolved, not denied.

05

Distance rations willingness as much as politics does.

Pakistan already has a real logistics bridge into the Gulf: troops, jets, and an air-defence system on Saudi soil since mid-2026. It has no comparable bridge into Anatolia. Turkey has basing in Qatar and a Gulf posture; it has none in South Asia. Geography sets a hard ceiling under any political willingness this model computes.

06

An India-Pakistan war is the case that breaks the pattern hardest.

Neither Riyadh nor Ankara has any incentive to be pulled into a nuclear-armed rivalry between two states with a decades-old territorial dispute. Expect diplomatic and rhetorical solidarity, not military entry, regardless of clause text.

07

Percentages describe judgment, not measurement.

Every percentage in this report is an estimative band, not a calculation from hard data: under 5 pc remote, 5 to 20 pc unlikely, 40 to 60 pc roughly a coin flip, 60 to 80 pc likely, above 90 pc almost certain. The full treaty text has never been made public, so none of this is confirmed against the actual document.

Where our own analysts disagree

This model is not a house consensus. In the working session that shaped it, one view held that India should not be overly concerned: the Pakistan-Turkey relationship is already substantial and unlikely to grow dramatically because of the new treaty, Saudi Arabia will likely maintain distance to protect its own India relationship, and fiscal constraints on both Pakistan and Turkey limit how much they can actually coordinate militarily against India.

The competing view held that even incremental military support for Pakistan has to be factored into India's planning regardless of how small it looks today, because formalising the treaty raises the scale of funding and technology transfer over time and compresses the window India has to achieve strategic gains in any future conflict. Both views are represented in this model. Neither has been resolved, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise.

This report is a working analytical model, not a forecast. The scenario and matrix figures rest on the original tracker built for this project; the four-mechanism breakdown, the case files, and the India-response tracks are analysis layered on top of that model rather than independently verified against outside sources. Treat every number here as an argument made legible, not a prediction.
